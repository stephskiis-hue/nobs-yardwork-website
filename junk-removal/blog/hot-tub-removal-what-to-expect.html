Hot tub removal: what actually happens on the day

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. Hot Tub Day

Published 30 June 2026 · 6 min read · No BS Junk Removal

A hot tub is the single most awkward object most homeowners will ever try to get rid of. It is heavy, it is waterlogged, it is wired into your house, and it is almost always in a back yard with a gate narrower than the tub. Here is exactly how the day goes when we do one.

Before we arrive: two things we need to know

When you get in touch, the two questions that decide everything are how we get to it and how it is powered.

Access means the narrowest point between the tub and our trailer — usually a side gate, sometimes a stretch between the house and the fence, occasionally a raised deck with no route down. A photo of that gap tells us more than any description. Power means whether it is a plug-in 120 V unit or hard-wired 240 V, because those are two different disconnect jobs.

The photo that gets you an accurate quote

One of the tub, one of the tightest gap it has to come through, and one from the street showing where a trailer could park. Three photos, two minutes, and the number we give you will hold.

Step one: draining

A full hot tub holds hundreds of gallons. It has to be empty before anything else happens, and draining is the one part with a real waiting time attached — often an hour or more on gravity alone.

This is the single most useful thing you can do before we arrive. If the tub is already drained when we get there, we start cutting instead of standing around watching a hose. If it is not, that is fine, we bring a pump — but it is time on the job either way.

Where the water goes matters. It should not go into the garden if it has been recently shocked with chemicals, and it should not be sent anywhere it will freeze into a skating rink across a walkway. In winter that second point stops being a nicety.

Step two: disconnecting

Power comes off at the breaker first, always. A plug-in unit is straightforward. A hard-wired 240 V tub is fed from its own breaker, usually through a disconnect box on the wall nearby.

We make the tub safe and remove it. What we do not do is pretend to be electricians — if the wiring needs to be properly terminated, capped and left to code inside your panel, that is an electrician's job and we will tell you so plainly. Most people want the feed left dead and capped so they can decide later whether to reuse the circuit for a shed or a garage.

Step three: cutting it down

Here is the thing nobody expects: the tub does not leave in one piece, and it is not supposed to.

A hot tub is an acrylic shell, a thick foam insulation layer, a plywood or composite cabinet, and a frame. We cut it into sections on site — typically quarters or eighths depending on the access — and carry the pieces out. It is loud for a while, it makes a mess of foam crumbs, and it is by far the safest way to do it.

The alternative that people imagine, tipping it on its edge and walking it out whole, is how fences get destroyed, how backs get injured, and how a 200 lb object ends up on somebody's foot. Cutting is not the shortcut. It is the professional method.

Step four: the mess

Foam gets everywhere. It is light, it is staticky, and a breeze will take it into your neighbour's yard if nobody is paying attention. Cleanup is part of the job, not an extra — we sweep, we collect the crumbs, and the pad the tub sat on should be visibly clean when we leave.

What you are usually left with is a concrete pad, a deck cut-out, or a rectangle of dead grass. If it is a pad you also want gone, say so at quote time, because that is heavy material and it is priced by weight rather than volume.

How long the whole thing takes

A straightforward tub with decent access, already drained: usually two to three hours on site. Add an hour if we are draining it. Add more if the only route out is through the house, over a deck, or around a fully-landscaped yard where we are protecting plantings on the way past.

It is a same-day job in essentially every case. You do not need to book time off for a multi-day project.

Where it ends up

The shell is not recyclable in any meaningful way and that part is honest landfill. The frame components, the pump, the heater and the wiring are metal and go to scrap. We do not claim a hot tub is a green job, because it mostly is not — the metal recovery is real, and the rest is not. That is the same standard we apply to everything else on the where your junk goes page.

One thing worth checking first

If the tub still runs, it may be worth more to you listed for free collection than it is as a removal job — someone will occasionally take a working tub away themselves. If it has been sitting dead for two winters with water in it, that ship has sailed, and it needs to come out before the frame rots into the deck.
