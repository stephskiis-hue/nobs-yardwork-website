Clearing a parent's house: a timeline that actually works

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. Estate Cleanouts

Published 16 June 2026 · 9 min read · No BS Junk Removal

Clearing out a parent's house is not a junk removal job with feelings attached. It is a grieving job that happens to involve a lot of furniture. Almost everyone who calls us about one is doing it for the first time, usually on a deadline, often while sorting out an estate at the same time. Here is the order that works.

Before anything leaves the house

There is one rule that matters more than every other piece of advice here: nothing goes out until the documents are found.

Wills, deeds, insurance policies, bank and investment paperwork, tax records, military service records, passports, birth and marriage certificates, safety deposit box keys. These are almost never in an obvious place. They are in a filing cabinet in the basement, taped inside a drawer, in a shoebox in the closet, or in an envelope inside a book.

The things people find too late

Cash in coat pockets and books. Jewellery in kitchen containers. Savings bonds and share certificates in with the Christmas cards. Photographs taped to the underside of drawers. Go through every drawer, pocket and book before the furniture leaves, not after.

A timeline that does not fall apart

Week one: find, do not decide

Walk the whole house and locate the paperwork and the obviously valuable items. Do not sort. Do not make keep-or-toss decisions. The only job this week is making sure nothing irreplaceable is sitting in a pile that somebody later assumes is rubbish.

Put everything found into one box, in one room, and tell everyone in the family which room that is.

Week two: the family walk-through

Get everyone who has a claim through the house, ideally together and in person. Sticky notes with names on them work better than any spreadsheet, because the argument happens in the room rather than three weeks later over text.

Where two people want the same thing, note it and move on. Do not try to resolve it standing in a hallway. The point of this pass is to find out what is genuinely wanted, which is almost always far less than people expect.

Week three: the four-pile sort

Now the actual sorting, room by room, one room finished before the next is started. Four categories only, because five is where people stall:

  • Family — claimed in the walk-through.
  • Sell — genuinely worth listing. Be ruthless here; most furniture is not.
  • Donate — usable, clean, someone else's problem solved.
  • Goes — everything else.

The most common failure is a fifth pile called “decide later”. It grows, it never shrinks, and it ends up being the reason the house is still full in November.

Week four: the haul

This is where we come in, and it is deliberately last. A crew arriving before the sorting is done means people making irreversible decisions under time pressure with strangers watching. That is when regret happens.

What we actually do on the day

We take the “goes” pile, and if you want, we handle the donation run too — usable furniture and household goods go to local reuse rather than to landfill wherever they can. That is standard, not an upsell. The routing is set out on the where your junk goes page.

We work room by room at whatever pace you set. If you need to stop and go through a box, we stop. Nobody is being timed, because the job is priced by volume rather than by the hour — which on this particular kind of job matters more than it sounds like it should. You are never paying for the minute you spend looking at a photograph.

The parts people underestimate

  • Volume. A three-bedroom house that has been lived in for forty years is not one trailer. Basements and garages in particular hold far more than they appear to.
  • Paper. Decades of documents, and much of it is sensitive. Set aside anything with account numbers, a health number or a signature for shredding rather than the general pile.
  • The garage and the shed. Old paint, solvents, fuel, pesticides and propane tanks are extremely common in them and we cannot legally take any of it. Separate that corner early and we will point you to who handles it.
  • How long it takes emotionally. Most people plan a weekend and need three. Booking the haul for a date you have already chosen forces the sorting to fit, which is either exactly the discipline you need or exactly the pressure you do not. You know which.

If it is a hoarding situation

Say so when you call. It changes the crew size, the equipment, the time and sometimes the safety precautions, and it is far better handled by people who knew before they arrived. There is no judgement in it — it is a category of job, not a verdict on anybody.

One thing worth saying

You are allowed to keep something that has no value to anyone but you, and you are allowed to let go of something valuable that you do not want. Neither decision needs justifying to a sibling, an appraiser, or us.
