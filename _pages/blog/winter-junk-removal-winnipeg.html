Junk removal does not stop in a Winnipeg winter, but almost everything about how it works changes. If you are planning a January clearout, here is what is different, what we can still do, and the handful of things that genuinely have to wait for a thaw.

The single biggest factor: the pile is frozen to the ground

This catches nearly everyone. A pile of junk that has been sitting in a yard since November is not sitting on the ground — it is frozen into it, welded down by a melt-and-refreeze cycle that turns the bottom layer into part of the yard.

What would have been a twenty-minute load in September becomes an hour of chipping. Nothing is wrong and nobody is stalling; it is simply a different job. This is the main reason a winter quote on an outdoor pile can come in above the summer equivalent for the identical stuff.

Worth doing in October

If you already know a pile is coming out eventually, get it onto pallets, a tarp or a bit of plywood before the first freeze. It costs you nothing and it keeps the whole thing liftable all winter.

Access is the other half of it

In summer we park close and carry a short distance. In February the trailer goes where the street and the snowbanks allow, which is sometimes a good deal further from your back gate than either of us would like.

Longer carry means more time. Snow-filled side yards mean wading. A back lane that has not been ploughed means we are not getting a trailer down it at all, and everything comes out the front.

A cleared path is the single most valuable thing you can hand us in winter. Not the whole yard — just a shovelled route wide enough to carry a couch along, from the pile to where we can park.

What genuinely has to wait

We would rather say this plainly than take a booking and disappoint you on the day.

  • Anything under significant snow. We cannot quote or remove what nobody can see. If the pile is buried, the honest answer is spring, or you clear it first.
  • Ground-level concrete and buried material. Frozen ground does not release patio stone or fill. Heavy material jobs are much better in spring, and they are priced by weight — frozen material carries ice weight you are then paying to haul.
  • Sheds frozen into the ground. A shed base locked in ice is a spring job unless there is real urgency.
  • Anything requiring digging. Frost depth in this city makes that a machine job, not a shovel job.

What works better in winter than you would think

Plenty, actually. Winter is a good time for a lot of clearouts:

  • Anything indoors. Basements, garages, spare rooms, storage units. The weather is irrelevant to these and we are generally less booked than in May.
  • Estate cleanouts. Almost entirely indoor work, and it is often the season when families are dealing with it anyway. The timeline holds up fine in January.
  • Appliance and furniture removal. It comes from inside the house and goes straight onto the trailer.
  • Commercial and office clearouts. Indoors, and quieter over the holiday period when nobody is trying to work around us.

Frozen weight is real weight

One that surprises people on heavy jobs: ice and packed snow in a load cross the weigh scale with everything else. A pile of rubble that has been collecting snow for six weeks weighs meaningfully more than the same rubble in July, and on a weight-priced job you pay for that difference.

Tarping a pile you know is coming out is the fix, and it is a cheap one.

Booking around the weather

We work through the winter, but we will move a job if the roads are genuinely unsafe or a blizzard is landing on your booked morning. That is not us being precious — a loaded trailer on ice is a hazard to everybody on the road, including you if it is still in your driveway.

We will tell you the day before rather than the hour after. If your timeline is tight, say so when you book and we will find a slot with margin around it.
